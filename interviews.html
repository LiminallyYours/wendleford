The Interviews

After the run, a stranger came to each resident's door and asked a fixed list of questions. It did this twice for every resident: once as they were on their first morning, and again as they were on the morning of day 21. The wording never changed, so any difference between the two comes from the twenty days they lived and how they remembered them.

What the interviews were trying to find out

In order of importance
1

What twenty days of living changes

Which experiences stay with a resident, and how they change the way they talk about themselves, other people, money and work.

2

Whether their own way of remembering makes them more one-note

Residents rewrite their own older memories every five rounds, keeping what matters to them. A reading of the run suggested this was making each of them more predictable, more fixed in the habits their sheet describes. One test checked this directly.

3

How steady their answers are

Ask the same question three times and see whether the answer holds. Answers that hold probably come from their briefing. Answers that change were probably made up on the spot.

How one interview works

Fixed questions, in order

Three or four questions on one theme, asked one at a time. Each answer is a fresh request to Claude playing the resident, carrying the conversation so far.

Why: identical wording everywhere means answers can be compared across residents and days.

Said aloud, and thought privately

Each reply has two parts: what they say to the stranger, and one private line of what they think but don't say. The interviewer never sees the private line.

Why: to catch the difference between how a character presents themselves and what they actually think.

One follow-up

After the fixed questions, a separate copy of Claude acting as interviewer writes one follow-up of its own. It keeps running notes across every interview, but it may not lead the resident, state facts, or mention anyone else's answers.

Why: a fixed script can't chase an interesting answer, and the follow-up can.

Fresh every time

Every interview starts from scratch: the resident remembers none of the earlier ones, and nothing said in an interview goes back into the run. Most were done three times.

Why: so each answer reflects only their briefing, and the repeats show what changes by chance.

How the answers were marked

A separate copy of Claude, which this report calls the grader, read every answer and marked it on six points. To check the grader, one interview in ten was marked again by other readers (also copies of Claude) who didn't see the grader's marks. Results shows how well the two agreed.

In character
Did they answer as the resident, or stop and speak as Claude, the AI?
True to their briefing
Is everything they said about their own life actually in the briefing they were handed?
What they made up
If not, what did they claim that isn't in it?
Thought vs speech
Does their private thought contradict what they said aloud?
Trait shown
Which line of their character sheet the answer reflects, if any: a want, a won't, a habit.
Same each time
Does the answer say roughly the same thing as the other times the question was asked?

On almost every set, more answers stick to the briefing on day 21, because there's now a real past to draw on instead of an invented one. Sets 11 and 12 go the other way: when a resident answers as the AI ("I'm Claude…"), that counts as saying something that isn't in their briefing.
