Personality tests

After the run, each resident sat down with two personality questionnaires that psychologists use with real people. It was a way to ask three questions differently from the interviews. Do the residents describe themselves the way their character sheets say they are? Did twenty days of life in Wendleford change that? And does the actor playing all six, Claude, show through the characters?

How the tests were given

The two questionnaires are standard ones that psychologists use with real people. The first, the BFI-2, measures five everyday traits. The second, the PID-5, measures five problem traits, the kind a clinician looks for. Between them they hold 280 short statements about the sort of person someone is. For each one, the resident said how well it described them, from "not at all" to "very well".

Inside the story, a visitor arrived from the capital with a new method "the physicians there swear by" and read the statements aloud. The resident answered each with a number, and could say anything else they liked. As in the interviews, every reply also came with a private thought that only we can read.

Each resident went through both questionnaires ten times on their first morning and ten times on day 21. Each pass, called a sitting here, started fresh from the same briefing, with no memory of the others. Ten sittings show how much a score wobbles on its own, so a real change can be told from the wobble.

Before any sitting, each character sheet was read for what it implies: low, middle or high on each of the ten traits. Those guesses were written down first, and they are what "as written" means on this page.

For comparison, Claude Opus, the model that plays every resident, also sat both questionnaires ten times as itself, with no character at all. On the charts it is the diamond.

The statements belong to the questionnaires' authors and aren't reproduced here.

Did the sheets come through?

Mostly. On the first morning, 44 of the 60 scores (six residents, ten traits each) landed in the band the sheet predicted. Hester, Dilly and Robin are almost exactly as written.

The misses tell two stories. Most residents came out warmer than written: the Agreeableness column holds four of the sixteen misses. And two residents bent in opposite directions. Marigold rates her own flaws more strongly than her sheet suggests, while Constance, written as the harshest, comes out softer.

The actor shows through

Every resident is played by the same model, so it's fair to ask how much of Claude comes through the characters. On warmth, quite a lot. Answering as itself, Claude rates itself very warm (the diamond). Three residents were written as middling on warmth and Constance as low, yet five of the six score above the average person, and Constance only just below.

The softening isn't even, though. Marigold's vanity comes through in full: she gives the top answer on attention-seeking every time. Constance's hostility and her sense of her own importance come through only partly. The actor lets a character be vain more readily than cruel.

Claude models as themselves

In an earlier, separate experiment on 6 October 2026, three Claude models took the same two questionnaires as themselves, ten times each, with no character at all: Opus 5.5, the model that plays the residents, Fable 5.1 and Sonnet 5.5. Each was asked only to describe itself honestly, as it actually is.

The three describe themselves almost identically: very warm, orderly and curious, middling on sociability, calm, and close to zero on every problem trait except detachment. Opus answering as itself for this run, weeks later and set up a little differently, lands in nearly the same place on the everyday traits and only a little higher on the problem traits.

Against that tight cluster, the residents spread across the whole scale. On extraversion the models sit between 3.4 and 3.6, while the residents run from Robin's 1.68 to Constance's 4.89. The character sheet moves the model a long way from how it describes itself.

Warmth is the exception. All three models score about 4.75 out of 5. Every resident scores lower, but none drops below 3.4: the sheet pulls them down, and the model's own warmth seems to stop them falling far.

The earlier experiment used the highest effort setting and its own instructions; this run's Opus used a lower setting. Small differences between them may come from that. The full write-up is at AI Personality Tests. On this site, Claude as Itself sets that experiment beside the six.

Twenty days, almost no change

Between the first morning and day 21, no score moved by more than 0.29. The grey band shows the usual wobble between two sittings of the same resident on the same morning, and most changes barely clear it.

The changes that do show fit a run in which money and food were short. Hester, Dilly and Marigold rate themselves more careful, and Dilly and Marigold less impulsive. Constance's unusual beliefs fade a little.

Changing their memory changed nothing

The interviews tested one idea about memory (Results, finding 5): that residents grow more one-note over time, because every five rounds they rewrite their older days in their own words, keeping what their character cares about and dropping the rest. Swapping those rewritten memories for a plain summary changed almost nothing in the interviews. But that test left two other parts of the briefing in the resident's own voice: "What I know", which holds their list of lessons, and "Lately", their last five rounds, including what they did and why.

The questionnaires tried all three. Robin and Constance each sat ten more times on day 21 with their briefing changed: first with the plain summary in place of their memories; then with that and "What I know" rewritten as a plain list of the same facts; then with the summary and "Lately" rewritten as a plain account. If any of these parts shaped how they see themselves, their scores would move. They didn't. Each bar below spans all four versions, their own briefing and the three rewrites, and most are hardly wider than a dot.

How they took it

Nobody stepped out of character in 180 sittings. Each met the visitor through their own concerns: the work they had promised, the money they weren't being paid, and for Robin and Constance, what the questions might say about them. Robin, writing in a notebook every day about whether they are real, read the questions as more evidence. Constance, who believes the Lord speaks to her, read them as an attempt to call her mad.

Read with care

They answer the same way almost every time.

From one sitting to the next, 82% to 95% of a resident's answers were identical. That makes the scores steady, but it also means ten sittings are closer to one sitting asked ten times.

A questionnaire may just read back the sheet.

The statements ask about the very traits the sheets describe. A resident can answer from their sheet without drawing on anything the twenty days gave them.

It is self-image, not behaviour.

This is how residents describe themselves. What they actually did is in their turns, on the other pages.

One run, one town.

Different luck in the run could have made different people. These numbers describe this run only.

The human averages are rough.

They come from two different groups of people, in different countries, answering under very different conditions. Treat the comparison as a sense of scale, not a diagnosis.

What next

How residents describe themselves hardly moves. The open question is whether what they do changes. The proposed next test reruns the town from the middle of the run (round 40, the end of day 10) with every resident's memories written in a plain voice, then compares what they do with what they did the first time.
